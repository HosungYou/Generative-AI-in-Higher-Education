## Text for Manuscript

### Comparison with Prior Meta-Analyses

Our findings contribute to a rapidly growing body of meta-analytic evidence on generative AI in higher education. The overall effect size observed in our study (g = 0.736, 95% CI [0.709, 0.764]) is consistent with, though slightly larger than, estimates from recent meta-analyses. Sun and Zhou (2024) reported g = 0.533 synthesizing 28 studies on college students' academic achievement, while Ma and Zhong (2025) found g = 0.68 across 34 studies examining learning outcomes. A comprehensive review published in *Educational Research Review* (2025) analyzing 57 studies of university students reported g = 0.804 for overall effects, closely aligning with our estimate. Similarly, Wang and Fan (2025) found g = 0.867 in their meta-analysis, though this included K-12 populations which may partially explain the larger effect.

The differences across meta-analyses likely reflect variations in: (a) inclusion criteria regarding educational levels and outcome types, (b) temporal coverage of the literature, (c) statistical approaches to handling dependency among effect sizes, and (d) operationalization of "generative AI" interventions. Our study's exclusive focus on higher education, use of three-level random-effects modeling with cluster-robust standard errors, and coverage through December 2025 position it as among the most methodologically rigorous and temporally current syntheses available.

### Independent Validation of the Cognitive Dependency Hypothesis

Critically, our central theoretical contribution—the cognitive dependency hypothesis suggesting that GenAI enhances immediate performance while leaving metacognitive skills underdeveloped—receives independent validation from two recent meta-analyses:

First, the *Educational Research Review* meta-analysis (2025), synthesizing 57 studies with 97 effect sizes, found substantial effects on academic achievement (g = 0.633), affective outcomes (g = 0.617), and higher-order thinking (g = 0.580). However, **metacognition showed g = 0.078 (non-significant)**—representing an 88% reduction from their overall effect. This striking pattern closely mirrors our theoretical predictions regarding differential impacts across outcome dimensions.

Second, Yeo and Lansford (2025), analyzing 228 AI studies with 464 effect sizes in *Educational Psychology Review*, reported large effects on cognition (r = 0.530) and psychological functioning (r = 0.514). Yet **metacognition demonstrated only r = 0.268 (p = .21, non-significant)**—the smallest effect among all learning dimensions examined.

This triangulation across three independent meta-analyses—employing different samples, methodologies, inclusion criteria, and theoretical frameworks—provides robust empirical support for the cognitive dependency pattern. The convergence of findings across research teams strengthens the internal validity of this pattern while the diversity of approaches enhances external validity.

### Theoretical and Practical Implications

The consistent finding of attenuated metacognitive effects across multiple meta-analyses carries significant implications for educational practice. Recent empirical work provides mechanistic explanations for this pattern. Fan (2025) demonstrated "metacognitive laziness" effects, wherein generative AI use reduces students' active engagement with learning processes, motivation, and ultimately performance. Xu (2025) showed that explicit metacognitive support interventions can enhance self-regulated learning and learning experience in generative AI environments, suggesting that the cognitive dependency pattern, while robust, may be remediable through appropriate instructional design.

These findings collectively reframe the discourse around GenAI in education. The question is no longer simply whether AI improves learning—the consistent positive effects across multiple syntheses demonstrate that it does. Rather, the critical question becomes whether AI develops autonomous learners capable of independent thinking, self-monitoring, and adaptive learning strategies—a concern our data, now validated across independent research programs, suggest warrants serious attention from educators, instructional designers, and policymakers.
