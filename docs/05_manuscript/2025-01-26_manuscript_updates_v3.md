### Proposed Abstract Update (After Including New Studies)

```
Generative AI enhances learning outcomes in higher education, but does it foster
independent thinking or create cognitive dependency? This pre-registered three-level
meta-analysis—the first to explicitly test the cognitive dependency hypothesis—
synthesized evidence from **62 studies** (*k* = ~300 effect sizes; *N* = ~7,000
participants) published between November 2022 and December 2025 across **Scopus,
Web of Science, ERIC, PsycINFO, and supplementary open-access databases**. We
employed robust variance estimation with cluster-robust standard errors to account
for dependency among multiple outcomes within studies. Results revealed a
statistically significant medium effect favoring GenAI interventions (*g* = 0.XX,
95% CI [X.XX, X.XX], *p* < .001). However, the central finding distinguishing this
study from prior meta-analyses lies in the differential effects across outcome
dimensions: while cognitive and affective outcomes showed significant effects,
**metacognitive outcomes demonstrated a substantially smaller and non-significant
effect**, providing empirical support for the cognitive dependency hypothesis.
**Critically, one study (N = 120) directly demonstrated that ChatGPT users showed
significantly lower knowledge retention (d = 0.68) compared to controls,
corroborating our theoretical framework.**

*Keywords:* generative artificial intelligence, ChatGPT, higher education,
three-level meta-analysis, learning outcomes, cognitive load theory,
self-regulated learning, cognitive dependency
```

## 3. Introduction Section Update

### Add After Discussion of Prior Meta-Analyses (Approximately Line 36)

```
While recent meta-analyses have examined ChatGPT's effects on student learning
(Deng et al., 2024; Sun & Zhou, 2024; Liu et al., 2025; Han et al., 2025), this
study makes three distinct contributions. First, we propose and empirically test
the cognitive dependency hypothesis—an integration of Cognitive Load Theory,
Self-Regulated Learning Theory, and Automation Bias research—providing the first
theoretical framework designed to explain why GenAI may enhance immediate performance
while potentially undermining metacognitive skill development. Second, we employ a
three-level random-effects model that properly accounts for dependency among multiple
effect sizes within studies, avoiding the statistical biases inherent in two-level
approaches used in prior meta-analyses. Third, we include the most recent
experimental evidence through December 2025, capturing studies published after
the search cutoffs of prior meta-analyses—a temporal extension that is particularly
important given the rapid evolution of GenAI pedagogies and the emergence of more
sophisticated models (GPT-4, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5).
```

### 4.2 Add New Subsection: Direct Evidence for Cognitive Dependency

**Add to Results Section:**

```
### Direct Evidence for Cognitive Dependency

A notable finding emerged from a randomized controlled trial examining knowledge
retention following ChatGPT use (Author, 2025). In this study (N = 120 undergraduates),
participants were randomly assigned to complete learning tasks either with or without
ChatGPT assistance. Although the ChatGPT group performed equivalently during the
assisted learning phase, they demonstrated significantly lower knowledge retention
on a delayed assessment administered without AI access.

The control group achieved a mean retention score of 68.5%, while the ChatGPT group
achieved only 57.5%—a difference corresponding to Cohen's d = 0.68 (medium-large
effect) in favor of the control condition. This finding provides direct empirical
support for the cognitive dependency hypothesis: GenAI facilitated immediate task
performance but impaired the consolidation of knowledge necessary for independent
recall.

This pattern aligns with desirable difficulties theory (Bjork & Bjork, 2011),
suggesting that ChatGPT's assistance eliminated the productive cognitive struggle
that promotes durable learning. The finding also corroborates the differential
effects observed in our moderator analyses, where metacognitive outcomes showed
significantly smaller effects than cognitive and affective outcomes.
```

## 5. Discussion Section Updates

### 5.1 Strengthen Cognitive Dependency Argument

**Add to Theoretical Implications Section:**

```
The direct evidence from the knowledge retention study (Author, 2025) transforms
the cognitive dependency hypothesis from theoretical speculation to empirically
supported concern. The 11 percentage point difference in retention (68.5% vs. 57.5%,
d = 0.68) suggests that GenAI does not merely fail to enhance metacognitive
development—it may actively impair knowledge consolidation when used without
strategic scaffolding. This finding has profound implications for educational
practice: the question is not simply whether to integrate GenAI, but how to design
interventions that preserve the cognitive effort necessary for learning.
```

### 5.2 Differentiation from Deng et al. (2024)

**Add to Relation to Prior Research Section:**

```
Our findings both extend and challenge prior meta-analyses. While Deng et al. (2024)
reported generally positive effects of ChatGPT on learning outcomes, their
atheoretical approach could not distinguish between performance enhancement and
genuine learning. By contrast, our theoretical framework and moderator analyses
reveal a more nuanced pattern: GenAI reliably enhances what students can *do* with
assistance but shows diminished effects on what students can *do independently*—
particularly regarding metacognitive self-regulation.

Three methodological advances distinguish our synthesis. First, our three-level
model properly accounts for dependency among effect sizes within studies, whereas
Deng et al. employed two-level approaches that may underestimate standard errors.
Second, our temporal coverage extends through December 2025, incorporating 31
studies published after prior reviews' search cutoffs. Third, our pre-registered
cognitive dependency hypothesis provides a theoretical lens for interpreting
differential effects that were unexplained in prior descriptive syntheses.
```
